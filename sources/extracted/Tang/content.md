## ARTICLE

# Do low-carbon cities hinder AI industry growth?

# Evidence from China

Luyuan Tang1 , Shiyao Xie2, Yuan Xu3✉ & Ziwen Sun4,5✉

As a crucial initiative to accelerate the green transition and development, China’s Low

Carbon City Pilot Policy (LCCP) has raised questions about its compatibility with the energy demand and carbon implications of artificial intelligence (AI) industrial growth. This study

treats the LCCP as a quasi-natural experiment and uses a staggered difference-in-differences (DID) framework to examine its relationship with urban AI industry development. The

analysis covers 285 Chinese prefecture-level cities from 2007 to 2022 and further examines

potential mechanisms and heterogeneity. The results indicate that the LCCP is associated

with higher AI industry development in pilot cities: in the fully specified model, the estimated

coefficient is 0.156, which corresponds to an approximately 16.9% higher level of AI enter prises relative to comparable non-pilot cities. Mechanism tests suggest that this relationship operates partly through improvements in energy consumption structure and green technol ogy innovation. Heterogeneity analysis further indicates that the policy effect varies across local contexts, with evidence of stronger effects in established innovation hubs and in some follower regions undergoing industrial transition. The study provides empirical evidence on

the compatibility of green and digital transitions and offers policy insights on how environ

mental regulation may be coordinated with high-tech regional development. https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-026-08090-2 OPEN

Introduction Climate change has become one of the most significant challenges of the 21st century, with urban areas standing as primary centres of human activity and major con tributors to global CO₂ emissions. Recent statistics show that cities contribute approximately 70% of global energy-related CO₂ emissions (Luqman et al., 2023). In China, this proportion is even more pronounced, with urban carbon emissions accounting for 70-80% of the national total, a figure some studies estimate could reach 85% by 2030 (Zhang et al., 2023). In response to this pressing environmental challenge, China has committed to ambitious ‘dual carbon’ targets, aiming for carbon neutrality, pledging to peak CO₂ emissions before 2030, and achieve carbon neutrality before 2060 (State Council Information Office, 2021). Simultaneously, the nation is actively promoting the development of the artificial intelligence (AI) sector, a cornerstone of its digital economy strategy (Cyranoski, 2018). However, the AI industry’s reliance on extensive computing facilities and data centres makes it inherently energy-intensive. This presents a significant policy challenge for China: reconciling stringent climate goals with the developmental needs of this strategically vital, yet high-consum ing, AI sector. China’s National Development and Reform Commission introduced the Low-Carbon City Pilot Policy (LCCP) to spear head its urban decarbonisation efforts and advance towards its national greenhouse gas reduction targets. Launched in phases starting from 2010, with subsequent expansions in 2012 and 2017, the LCCP aims to explore replicable regional low-carbon development models, taking into account diverse regional development stages, resource endowments, and industrial struc tures. Given the strategic importance of the AI industry and its significant energy demands against the backdrop of these envir onmental initiatives, a critical question forms the core of this paper: Do these Low-Carbon City policies, designed to curtail urban carbon footprints, inadvertently hinder or otherwise shape the growth of the AI industry? A strong consensus in the literature has established the LCCP’s significant impact on emissions reduction, even after accounting for economic cycles and other confounding factors(Hou et al., 2023; Z Li et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2023). Existing research has moved beyond its direct climate impacts to assess its broader economic consequences: empirical studies have shown the policy has improved enterprise Total Factor Productivity (TFP) (Ren et al., 2022); fostered technological advancement through opti mised resource allocation and reduced financing constraints (Zhu and Lee, 2022); and critically, begun to reshape local industrial landscapes by inhibiting high-carbon industries while stimulating emerging sectors (Li et al., 2023). This established role as a restructuring tool, rather than merely a regulatory constraint, raises a crucial question about its effect on the AI industry. However, these studies have predominantly focused on tradi tional industries or general economic indicators, leaving this question largely unanswered. This research gap is particularly critical because the AI industry presents a unique duality: its technologies can optimise energy systems and enhance resource efficiency, thereby contributing to environmental governance (Tao et al., 2024). Yet, its operations, particularly data centres and extensive computing, generate substantial carbon footprints, a concern amplified by rising global energy-related CO₂ emissions, which increased by 1.1% in 2023 to a historic high (IEA, 2024). While some nascent research explores AI’s role in carbon reduction, the specific influence of low-carbon city policies on AI industry development remains systematically unexamined. This void makes it challenging to ascertain whether environmental policies stifle emerging industries, how to recon cile emission reduction targets with industrial development needs, and whether policy design must account for industry-specific characteristics. This study addresses this gap by examining how the LCCP is associated with AI industry growth in China. Rather than assuming that environmental regulation necessarily imposes a binding constraint on technology-intensive sectors, our empirical results suggest that the LCCP is positively related to AI industry development in pilot cities. Theoretically, this research extends the analytical framework linking environmental regulation and industrial development by focusing on the interaction between environmental policy and the digital economy. It also considers the heterogeneous energy-use characteristics of different seg ments of the AI industry chain. Practically, the study provides evidence on the mechanisms and heterogeneous effects through which low-carbon policies may shape a strategic emerging industry. These findings can inform policymakers seeking to coordinate low-carbon development with industrial innovation while designing differentiated regional support measures. The subsequent chapters are organised as follows: Section 2 introduces the policy background and presents the research hypotheses; Section 3 describes the research design; Section 4 reports the main empirical results; Section 5 presents the mechanism analysis; Section 6 introduces the heterogeneity analysis; and finally, Section 7 draws the research conclusions based on the previous studies and provides theoretical implica tions and policy recommendations.

Research background and hypotheses development The implementation of low-carbon policies represents China’s strategic choice in addressing rapidly increasing carbon emissions and promoting green transformation. China, the world’s largest carbon emitter (Yang et al., 2020), urgently needed to transform its development model. In this context, China launched three rounds of low-carbon city pilot programmes in July 2010, November 2012, and February 2017. The first round consisted of five provinces (Guangdong, Liaoning, Hubei, Shaanxi, and Yun nan) and eight cities (Tianjin, Chongqing, Shenzhen, Xiamen, Hangzhou, Nanchang, Guiyang, and Baoding). The second round included 28 cities, such as Beijing, Shanghai, and Qinhuangdao, along with Hainan Province. The third round, building on the successes of the previous two, covered 45 cities (districts and counties). Due to the extensive scope of the third round, pilot regions were required to develop replicable and scalable experi ences, with successful practices gradually being promoted nationwide by 2020. Therefore, this study considers 2010, 2012, and 2017 as the implementation time points for the low-carbon city pilot policy.

Hypothesis development: the impact of Low-carbon cities on artificial intelligence industry development. The impact of environmental regulations on industrial development, in eco nomic theory, is fundamentally manifested as a strategic trade-off between compliance costs and innovation offsets (Z Li et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2023). This theoretical framework provides a fun damental analytical perspective for understanding how low carbon city pilot policies influence the AI industry, a frontier sector. Environmental regulations may generate a cost effect that inhibits industrial development (Antoniou and Mageiropoulos, 2024). This viewpoint contends that environmental regulations impose additional burdens that create negative impacts on enterprises. For traditional industries, this manifests as direct compliance costs like pollution taxes or investments in end-of pipe treatment, which can crowd out productive investments (Hafstead and Williams, 2018; Song et al., 2021). For technology intensive sectors like the AI industry, which are characterised by high energy consumption for data centres and computational processes, this cost effect operates more indirectly through rising energy prices. Environmental regulations, by increasing the cost of fossil fuel-based energy, can elevate operational expenditures for AI firms, potentially dampening their expansion and innovation capacity. Well-designed environmental regulations can stimulate an innovation offset effect, namely the Porter Hypothesis (Lanoie et al., 2008; Wanli Zhang et al., 2024). This hypothesis posits that stringent yet flexible environmental regulations can compel enterprises to engage in technological innovation, and the benefits derived from such innovation—including efficiency improvements and new market development—can not only offset but even exceed the initial compliance costs, while creating new competitive advantages for firms (Porter and Linde, 1995). This innovation offset constitutes the core logic through which environmental regulations promote industrial structural upgrading (Chong et al., 2017), transforming environmental pressures into internal incentives for enterprises to pursue cleaner and more efficient production methods, thereby achieving a win-win outcome for both economic and environmental objectives. The balance between these competing effects—the cost-driven inhibition and the innovation-driven offset—is contingent upon the specific design of the environmental policy and the institutional context in which it is implemented. In the context of China’s low-carbon city pilots, we argue that the policy architecture is intentionally designed to favour the Porter effect, causing innovation offsets to likely exceed compliance costs. As a large developing country, China often takes a gradual reform approach to environmental regulation, using a pilot-first strategy that grants local governments greater autonomy in policy implementation (Chen et al., 2024). In this framework, environ mental regulation generally pairs with supporting industrial policies, creating a dual policy combination of environmental constraints plus innovation incentives. As a comprehensive reform initiative, the low-carbon city pilot establishes carbon emission constraints and offers innovation incentives such as fiscal subsidies, tax breaks, and technical support, which proactively reduce the risks and costs of enterprise innovation, directly facilitating the emergence of the Porter effect and effectively mitigating the potential innovation-inhibiting effects that might arise from basic environmental regulations. Further more, under the performance evaluation system, local govern ments in China often leverage environmental regulation as a catalyst for industrial upgrading, facilitating the concentration of high-tech industries in their regions through a mix of investment promotion and industrial initiative policies. Specifically, this unique policy environment combining con straints with targeted incentives and driven by local upgrading agendas is anticipated to overcome potential revisionist barriers and foster AI industry development primarily through two interconnected pathways: first, by reshaping the energy con sumption structure through differential cost signals and support for renewables, thereby attracting energy-sensitive high-tech firms; and second, by stimulating green technology innovation, generating knowledge spillovers that lower adoption thresholds for technology-intensive sectors like AI. Based on this theoretical analysis, which integrates the potential mechanisms within the specific Chinese context, this study proposes the following core hypothesis: H1: The low-carbon city pilot policy can foster the develop ment of the artificial intelligence industry. The effect of the low-carbon city pilot policy on the artificial intelligence industry primarily operates through the cost transmission mechanism, shaped by environmental regulation constraints (leading to changes in energy consumption structure), and the knowledge spillover mechanism associated with techno logical externalities (stemming from green technology innova tion). According to the new economic geography theory (Krugman, 1992) and technology spillover theory, environmental regulation can enhance industrial development advantages by optimising energy structures and promoting green technology innovation.

Hypothesis development: the mediating role of energy con sumption structure. As the central subject of carbon emissions within the urban economic system, the low-carbon transforma tion of enterprises directly influences the effectiveness of urban environmental governance. From a cost-based theoretical per spective, environmental regulatory policies have raised the prices of traditional energy sources, encouraging artificial intelligence companies to seek lower-cost energy alternatives. This shift optimises their green energy consumption structure and sig nificantly enhances the geographical clustering of the industry. Specifically, on one hand, the low-carbon city pilot policy has notably increased the costs associated with traditional energy sources like coal-fired power through mechanisms such as carbon emission rights trading, environmental taxes, and differentiated electricity pricing. On the other hand, the policy has spurred the growth of renewable energy capacity and technological advancements through the green power quota system and priority dispatch mechanism, leading to a continuous reduction in the marginal costs of clean energy sources like wind and solar power. As the supply of green energy reaches a critical scale, its low marginal cost characteristics will reshape the regional power market dynamics, resulting in a competitive decline in low carbon energy prices. This structural change exerts a significant gravitational influence on the artificial intelligence industry, which is not only highly electricity-intensive due to the demands of data centres and model training, but also highly sensitive to the stability and predictability of its energy supply. When pilot areas develop a dual advantage in both competitive green electricity pricing and enhanced grid stability, both upstream and down stream enterprises within the artificial intelligence industry chain will naturally form spatial clusters based on cost efficiency and operational stability. Accordingly, this paper proposes: H2: The low-carbon city pilot policy changes the energy consumption structure to attract the agglomeration and devel opment of the artificial intelligence industry.

Hypothesis development: the mediating role of green tech nology innovation. In addition to influencing industrial agglomeration by altering the energy consumption structure, environmental regulation can also impact the path of technology diffusion by stimulating innovation activities. From the perspective of innovation compensation, moderate environmental regulation can encourage enterprises to pursue green technology innovation, and its knowledge spillover effect can lower the technology adoption threshold for related industries, thereby facilitating the industry’s growth. Specifically, there are notable differences between green technology innova tion and traditional technology innovation, as green innovation often exhibits stronger universality and positive externality characteristics (Hu et al., 2022). Methodology Data sources. The study covers data from 2007 to 2022, including 285 prefecture-level and higher cities in China. The temporal scope of the analysis is intentionally constrained to this period to ensure the validity of the causal identification strategy. The pri mary methodological concern is the global proliferation of gen erative AI in 2023, which constitutes a major exogenous economic shock. The effects of this shock are expected to be highly asymmetric, disproportionately impacting cities with strong technological infrastructure and human capital. Including data from 2023 would therefore introduce a direct violation of the parallel trends assumption, a foundational requirement for the DID and related quasi-experimental methods used in this ana lysis. Consequently, it would be impossible to disentangle the estimated effect of the low-carbon city policy from the hetero geneous economic impacts of the AI shock, introducing sub stantial bias into the treatment effect estimates. Furthermore, the 2022 endpoint aligns with the availability of complete and verified annual data from our primary sources at the time of collection. To ensure data integrity and the robustness of our findings, cities with missing data for more than 30% of the observations were excluded from the final sample. (Jiang et al., 2024). Data on AI industry development were obtained from the Chinese enterprise credit information platform, ‘Qichacha’ (Xu et al., 2023), where we extracted and verified information on 3,827,038 AI-related enterprises based on their registered industry codes and business descriptions. To address potential data quality issues, we implemented a three-step data cleaning process: (1) removing duplicate entries by cross-checking company registra tion numbers and names; (2) validating enterprise locations through geocoding verification; and (3) excluding enterprises with inconsistent or implausible founding dates. The low-carbon city pilot list was sourced from relevant National Development and Reform Commission documents (Liu and Qin, 2016), with implementation dates carefully verified against local government announcements. Additional socio economic indicators came from the Express Professional Superior (EPS) (Wenqiu Zhang et al., 2024) platform and the ‘China City Statistical Yearbook’ (Desmet and Rossi-Hansberg, 2013). For minor missing data points (less than 5% of observations in any city), linear interpolation was employed between adjacent years, while structural missing data patterns were addressed using multiple imputation techniques (Chen et al., 2023). To account for potential outliers, we applied winsorisation at the 1st and 99th percentiles for continuous variables (Boudt et al., 2020).

Dependent variable: development of artificial intelligence industry (DAII). To measure the development levels of the AI industry, following the methodology of (HKPC and HIEBS, 2024) and (Sun et al., 2022), we utilised Python web crawling tech nology to perform fuzzy keyword matching queries on the ‘business scope’ and ‘company name’ fields in the Qichacha database. We employed keywords related to AI applications, including ‘artificial intelligence,’‘cloud,’‘data,’‘IoT,’ and ‘machine learning.’ The data was aggregated by year and region to create panel data of AI enterprises in Chinese cities from 2007 to 2022. The natural logarithm of the number of AI enterprises in each city-year observation was employed to measure AI industry development (DAII).

Independent variable: low-carbon city pilot policy (LCCP). We employed the staggered Difference-in-Differences (DID) method to examine the implementation effects of the low-carbon city pilot policy (Pei et al., 2019; Popp, 2006). When the pilot region was a province, all cities within that province were regarded as pilot cities. If the same city appeared in different batches of pilot lists, the earliest batch was utilised to establish the pilot start time. Addressing selection bias with PSM-DID. A critical assumption for the validity of the DID model is the parallel trends assump tion, which posits that the treatment and control groups would have followed similar trends in the absence of the policy. How ever, the selection of cities for the low-carbon pilot programme may not be random, potentially leading to a systematic difference between the groups and a violation of this assumption. This creates a potential sample selection bias. To address this issue and enhance the robustness of our findings, we employ the Propensity Score Matching DID (PSM-DID) method for re-estimation (Heckman et al., 1997). We use a logit model to estimate the propensity score for each city being selected as a pilot, based on the control variables. Then, we use one-to-one nearest neighbour matching to construct a new control group that is statistically similar to the treatment group. This approach effectively miti gates selection bias and strengthens the validity of our DID analysis.

IV estimations. Reverse causality is a significant factor con tributing to the endogeneity problem. Specifically, the low-carbon city pilot policy is not entirely exogenous, as the development needs of the regional artificial intelligence industry may, in turn, encourage the implementation of the policy, forming a two-way causal relationship. To address this, the paper uses the instru mental variable method in the benchmark regression. It con structs two sets of geographical-historical instrumental variables. First, data from government reports of 285 prefecture-level cities from 2008 to 2022 are manually collected, segmented, and ana lysed to count the frequency of words related to environmental regulation and protection. The proportion of these words relative to the total word count in each government report is used as an instrumental variable (IV1). The frequency of environmental regulation and protection words indicates local governments’ attention to environmental issues, which is partly independent of the implementation effects of the low-carbon city pilot policy. Hence, this instrumental variable meets the exclusivity criterion. Additionally, it is strongly correlated with the policy’s imple mentation, as increased attention to environmental issues by local authorities boosts the likelihood of actively promoting the low carbon city pilot policy, satisfying the relevance criterion and making it a suitable instrumental variable. The low-carbon city pilot policy fosters green technology innovation and supports the growth of the artificial intelligence industry. This severity often influences local governments’ attention to and execution of the low-carbon city pilot policy. Results The impact of LCCP on the development of the artificial intelligence industry. Based on the benchmark model, this section examines the quantitative association between the low-carbon city pilot policy (LCCP) and urban AI industry development. The baseline estimates are presented in Table 2. In column (1), without control variables and fixed effects, the LCCP coefficient is positive and significant at the 1% level. After controlling for city character istics and two-way fixed effects in columns (2) and (3), the coeffi cient remains positive and statistically significant. In the fully specified model in column (3), the LCCP coefficient is 0.156. Since DAII is measured as the natural logarithm of the number of AI enterprises, this estimate implies that, all else equal, pilot cities have an approximately 16.9% higher level of AI industry development than non-pilot cities (exp(0.156)-1). This finding supports H1 and suggests that the LCCP is positively associated with urban AI industry growth.

Parallel trend tests are conducted on pilot cities and non pilot cities. The results of the parallel trend test confirm the validity of our difference-in-differences approach. As illustrated in Fig. 2, before the LCCP implementation, the regression coef ficients did not demonstrate statistical significance, indicating no notable difference in AI industry development trajectories between pilot cities and non-pilot cities. This confirms that the control and treatment groups followed similar trends prior to the policy intervention, satisfying the crucial parallel trend assumption. Following the policy implementation, the coefficients began to show a general upward trend, aligning with our main regression results and suggesting a positive influence of the policy on AI industry development. The improvement in AI industry growth became notably greater in the treated cities compared to the control cities. Importantly, statistical significance was achieved from the second year (D2) after the policy enactment, indicating a potential one-year lag effect of LCCP. This lag effect likely stems from the natural delay between policy announcement and full implementation, or it could reflect the adjustment period cities needed to respond to and leverage the new policy framework. The sustained significance of coefficients in subsequent periods further confirms the robustness of the LCCP’s positive impact on AI industry development.

Using instrumental variables to address endogeneity issues. To rigorously address potential endogeneity issues arising from reverse causality between low-carbon policies and AI industry growth, we employ a two-stage least squares(2SLS) instrumental variable estimation. Table 3 reports the empirical results of this strategy using two distinct historical-geographical instruments: local government environmental rhetoric intensity (IV1) and pre policy industrial pollution stocks (IV2). As presented in the first stage regressions (columns 1 and 3), both instruments display strong relevance in predicting LCCP implementation. Specifically, a higher frequency of environmental words in government reports (IV1) is significantly and positively associated with being selected as a pilot city (0.151\*\*, t = 2.08). Similarly, historical industrial pollution stocks (IV2) exhibit a strong positive linkage with the subsequent policy enforcement (0.164\*\*\*, t = 4.82), satisfying the relevance criterion of valid instruments. In the second-stage estimations (columns 2 and 4), the instrumented LCCP maintains a positive and statistically significant coefficient at the 1% level across all model specifications (3.570\*\*\* for IV1 and 4.009\*\*\* for IV2). Referring to the industry-level division paradigm officially proposed by Qichacha, the artificial intelligence industry chain is divided into three segments: the basic layer (upstream link), the technical layer (midstream link), and the application layer (downstream link). Specifically, the basic layer includes the three pillars of computing power infrastructure, core hardware components, and the data resource system; the technical layer focuses on key technical modules such as algorithm research and development, platform construction, and tool development; the application layer concerns the development of innovative terminal products and the implementation of industry scenario solutions. In identifying the categories of sample enterprises, this paper constructs a text classification algorithm model based on the QWEN-7B large language model (Bai et al., 2023) and leverages natural language processing technology to extract semantic features and recognise patterns in the business scope text of the enterprise, ultimately achieving precise positioning and hierarchical attribution determination of the sample enterprises within the industrial chain. By analysing data from sample enterprises, it is observed that the upstream sector primarily comprises high-emission companies dependent on traditional energy sources. Since basic hardware manufacturing and large-scale data centre operations necessitate a continuous and stable supply of significant electricity, they predominantly rely on conventional energy sources, such as coal fired power. The midstream sector can partially utilise renewable energy for technological research and development, as well as platform operations, but it still requires assurances from traditional energy sources for computing-intensive tasks. The downstream sector mainly consists of low-emission enterprises that utilise clean energy, since terminal applications have relatively low energy demands, and the companies’ images and ESG requirements compel them to use clean energy to meet carbon neutrality objectives. In contrast, midstream technology-focused companies are restricted by the path dependency of high-precision algorithm research and development on a stable energy supply, facing dual pressures from equipment upgrades and rising R&D costs in the short term, which leads to the variability of policy transmission.

We categorise the sample cities into three groups for heterogeneity analysis: first-tier, second-tier, and third-tier (and below) cities, according to (Fan et al., 2021). The results, presented in Columns (1)–(3), indicate that the policy significantly promotes the AI industry in first-tier and third-tier cities, but not in second-tier cities. In comparison, third-tier cities, with less industrial path depen dency, can utilise the policy as an opportunity. Second-tier cities, by contrast, may be lacking the cutting-edge advantages of the former and the flexibility of the latter. This suggests the policy acts as a powerful catalyst for creative destruction. In industry heavy cities, constraints on traditional sectors compel local actors to invest in new, sustainable growth engines like AI, accelerating diversification. For cities with an already diversified or service oriented economy, this transformative pressure is less acute, resulting in a weaker policy response.

Discussion This paper conducts an empirical study on the impact of LCCP on the development of the artificial intelligence industry. The results provide crucial implications for both theory and practice. Our results suggest that low-carbon city pilot policies are positively associated with the development of the artificial intel ligence industry. This finding is consistent with research showing that environmental regulatory policies can promote corporate technological innovation by constraining high-pollution emis sions (Yang et al., 2023) and with the view that environmental regulation can affect corporate productivity and competitiveness (Shang et al., 2023). Our research extends this perspective to strategic emerging industries, showing that environmental poli cies may influence not only traditional sectors but also high-tech industries. This is relevant because high-tech industries such as AI are not typically viewed as primary targets of environmental regulation. The results suggest that, when green policies are designed and implemented in ways that influence energy struc ture and induce green innovation, they may generate positive spillovers for advanced technological sectors. Furthermore, our heterogeneity analysis adds to the literature on AI development by identifying a policy-related channel that complements established market-driven factors such as techno logical progress (Rashid and Kausik, 2024), talent aggregation (Zhang et al., 2023), and capital investment (Babina et al., 2024). Rather than suggesting that environmental regulation replaces these factors, our findings indicate that strategic environmental policy can shape the local conditions under which AI industry growth occurs. Crucially, this policy effect is not uniform. The significant effects observed in established hubs, including first-tier cities and eastern regions, suggest that the policy may reinforce accumu lated innovation advantages. The significant effects observed in some less-developed or transition-oriented areas, including wes tern regions, third-tier cities, and heavy-industry cities, are consistent with a policy-induced upgrading mechanism. This evidence does not imply that all follower regions can auto matically leapfrog into high-tech sectors. Rather, it suggests that, under certain local conditions, environmental policy shocks may help loosen industrial path dependence and create opportunities for entry into digital-economy activities. Consequently, the study broadens the definition of an enabling environment for AI by adding green policy design to the more commonly discussed factors of capital, talent, and research infrastructure. A crucial nuance revealed by our structural analysis is the asymmetric response within the AI industry chain, particularly the potential transitional friction faced by the midstream tech nical layer. Unlike downstream applications that feature higher energy flexibility, midstream tasks such as continuous model training and computational optimization are structurally rigid in their energy demand. A rapid transition away from traditional fossil-fuel energy sources, if mismatched with the immediate scalability of high-stability renewable grids, may introduce subtle operational uncertainties or cost adjustments for technology focused enterprises. Rather than undermining our primary hypothesis, this friction underscores the complexity of regional green-digital coordination, demonstrating that the ‘Porter effect’ is not frictionless but requires industry-specific policy scaffolding. Our findings suggest that environmental regulation can be con sidered not only as a constraint but also as a potential component of industrial policy when it is coordinated with innovation and energy policies. To maximise co-benefits for the AI industry, policy design could be optimised to strengthen the synergies identified in this study. First, policymakers could strategically leverage the two key channels highlighted by the analysis: green innovation and energy market signals. This may involve fostering R&D collaboration between climate-tech and AI firms and using market-based instru ments, such as time-of-use electricity pricing, where local conditions support such measures. Second, policy implementation should be adaptive and context-specific rather than uniform. For follower regions, green mandates may create opportunities for industrial renewal and new investment in digital infrastructure. Third, to fur ther refine the efficiency of environmental regulations, policy implementation should be adaptive, context-specific, and structurally differentiated across the industry chain rather than uniform. To maximize co-benefits and mitigate transitional frictions, policymakers should implement targeted green power security protocols for energy-intensive midstream AI enterprises. This includes establishing dedicated green energy direct-supply channels, optimizing time-of use pricing frameworks for continuous large-scale model training, and providing specialized technical subsidies to accelerate the deployment of intelligent microgrids and distributed computing facilities. Such measures will successfully safeguard the stability of core high-tech research while advancing urban decarbonization goals. Our study’s conclusions should be considered in light of several important limitations. First, the analysis is based on city-level data, which allows us to evaluate broad regional patterns but does not capture firm-level adjustment, entry, exit, or productivity responses. Second, AI industry development is measured using enterprise registration data and keyword-based identification from Qichacha; although data-cleaning procedures were imple mented, this measure may not fully capture the technological depth, employment scale, patent quality, or commercial perfor mance of AI firms. Third, although the staggered DID design, parallel-trend test, placebo exercise, PSM-DID estimation, and instrumental-variable analysis help mitigate endogeneity con cerns, they cannot completely eliminate all possible biases arising from unobserved time-varying city characteristics or overlapping industrial policies.
